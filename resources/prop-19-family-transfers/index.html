Proposition 19 Family Transfers

How California's Proposition 19 changed parent-to-child home transfers, why the home's market value on the transfer date now matters, and where a private appraisal fits in.

What changed with Proposition 19

Before February 16, 2021, California children who received a parent's home usually kept the parents' low tax base. Proposition 19 narrowed that. Now the exclusion generally applies only to the family home, and only if the child moves in and makes it their principal residence. Rentals and vacation homes are generally reassessed. Even for the family home, there is a cap. Then the child can keep the parents' taxable value only up to a limit tied to that value plus about $1 million, an amount adjusted periodically for inflation. If the home's market value is higher than that limit, the difference is added to the taxable value. So the market value on the transfer date matters.

Why market value on the transfer date matters

The assessor compares the home's market value on the date of transfer with the parents' taxable value plus the allowed amount. For an inheritance, the date of transfer is generally the date of death. For a gift during the parents' lifetime, it is generally the date the deed is recorded. A higher value means more may be added. The assessor will reach its own opinion of value. A well-supported private appraisal gives your family an independent number for the same date. If the assessor's figure looks too high, you have documented evidence to discuss with them or to use in an appeal. Without it, you are arguing with a figure you cannot check.

The filing side

Claims for the parent-child exclusion are filed with the San Diego County Assessor, generally on the state form BOE-19-P. The child also needs to file for the homeowners' exemption within the time allowed to show the home is now their principal residence. There are deadlines, and missing them can cost the exclusion, so confirm them with the assessor or your attorney. An appraisal is not required to file the claim, but many families and their advisers want one. It helps with planning before a transfer, with estate accounting after one, and with any discussion with the assessor about value. A CPA may also use the date-of-death value to establish the heirs' income tax basis.

Planning before a transfer

If parents are still living and considering a transfer, a current appraisal can show how close the home is to the Proposition 19 limit. That can affect whether a lifetime gift, a sale, a trust plan or simply waiting makes more sense. These are legal and tax decisions, and your attorney or CPA should guide them. The appraisal gives them a real number to work with instead of an online estimate. A Desktop appraisal is often enough for early planning. When the transfer actually happens, or if the value is likely to be close to the limit, a Standard report with an interior inspection provides stronger support. Brian can talk through which report fits your stage of the process.

What the appraisal report will include

For a Proposition 19 matter, the report states the effective date, usually the date of death or the date of transfer, and that the intended use is property tax and estate planning. The report describes how the home stood on that date, lists the comparable sales and walks through every adjustment. The final value is stated as market value as of the effective date. If the appraisal is retrospective, older listings, photos and permits help describe the home accurately. If the home needed major repairs on the transfer date, document it, because that can lower the value and the amount added to the tax base. Share what you have, and the report will explain how it was used.
