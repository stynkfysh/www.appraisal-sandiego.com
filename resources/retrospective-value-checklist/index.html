Retrospective Value Checklist

What to gather when you need a home's value for an earlier date, such as a date of death or date of separation, and how the appraiser uses it.

What a retrospective appraisal is

A retrospective appraisal gives a home's market value as of a date in the past. Estates and trusts often need the date-of-death value, which can set heirs' tax basis. Divorces may need the value on the date of separation. The question is what the home would most likely have sold for that day, in its condition then. That is different from today's value in two ways. First, the comparable sales have to come from around the effective date, not from this month. Second, the home has to be described as it was then. A kitchen remodeled after the date of death should not raise the value, and damage that happened later should not lower it.

Pin down the effective date

Before anything else, confirm the exact date the value should apply to. For an estate, that is usually the date of death on the death certificate. For a trust, it may be the date of death of the trustor or another date in the trust document. For a divorce, your attorney will tell you which date applies. Sometimes more than one date matters. An estate may want both the date-of-death value and a current value for a planned sale. Divorces sometimes need values at two points in time. One assignment can usually cover both. Tell us every date you need when you ask for a quote.

Gather what the home looked like then

The appraiser needs to know the home's condition and features on the effective date. Helpful items include old listing photos, family photos taken around that time, insurance documents, permits, contractor invoices and any earlier appraisals or inspection reports. If the home has been changed since, a short written list of what was done and when is very useful. Be honest about condition. If the roof leaked, the kitchen was original or a room was unfinished, say so. The appraiser cannot see the past, so your information and the records fill in the gaps. The report will note what was assumed, which protects everyone if the value is later questioned by an heir, the other side or a tax authority.

How the comparable sales are chosen

For a retrospective value, Brian looks for sales that closed shortly before the effective date, plus pending or recent sales that show the market's direction at that time. A sale that closed after the date can sometimes help, but it is used carefully and explained. Sales from years later generally do not reflect what a buyer would have paid on the effective date. Market conditions are part of the analysis. If prices were rising or falling around the date, the adjustments reflect that. In San Diego County, the market has moved quickly at times, so a date even a few months away can matter. The report explains how the market was trending and why the chosen comparables fit.

Inspection or desktop for a past date

If the home still looks largely the way it did on the effective date, an inspection helps confirm size, layout and quality. If it has been sold, remodeled or damaged since, a desktop appraisal using records and photos may be the more practical route. Either way, the report states how the home was observed and what was assumed about its condition on the effective date. For high-stakes matters such as contested estates or litigation, an inspection plus strong documentation usually gives the most defensible result. For straightforward estates where heirs agree and the home has not changed, a desktop report is often enough. Your attorney or CPA may have a preference.
