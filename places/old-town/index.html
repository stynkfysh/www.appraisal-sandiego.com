Old Town

Where San Diego started, with hillside homes and condos near the transit center

San Diego began here. Spanish soldiers and missionaries set up the Presidio on the hill above in 1769, and the town that grew below it became the first non-Native settlement in what is now California. Old Town San Diego State Historic Park keeps many of the adobe and frame buildings from the Mexican and early American periods.

Most people think of Old Town as a place to visit, but people live here too. Homes and condos sit on the hillsides above San Diego Avenue and near the Old Town Transit Center, where the trolley, Coaster and Amtrak meet. The area is small and sales are few, which makes picking comparables harder. We often pull from nearby Mission Hills, Bay Park and Point Loma when there are not enough local sales.

The housing stock

Old Town's housing is a mix of small single-family homes on the slopes, apartment buildings and condo complexes, and some townhome projects near the transit center and along Taylor Street. Many hillside houses date from the 1920s to the 1950s and sit on steep or irregular lots. The Old Town San Diego Planned District sets design rules and a height limit meant to keep the historic character, so new construction is limited. Some condos and townhomes were built from the 1970s onward. Lots near the San Diego River and the flatter western blocks can carry different flood and soils considerations than the hillsides.

Value drivers to watch around Old Town

01

Planned district rules

The Old Town planned district controls building height, style and materials. That limits redevelopment and additions, but it also protects the look of the area. We consider how these rules affect a buyer's plans and compare to sales with similar constraints.

02

Few local sales

With a small number of homes, there may be only a few sales in a given year. We widen the search to nearby areas with similar housing and adjust carefully for location. The report explains why each comp was chosen.

03

Transit and tourism

Being near the transit center is handy for commuters, but tour buses, event crowds and freeway noise from Interstates 5 and 8 can be drawbacks. We look at exactly where the home sits relative to these features.

04

Low areas near the river

Parcels on the flatter ground toward the San Diego River should be checked against FEMA flood maps, since a flood zone designation affects insurance costs and buyer interest. We check the map for the subject and each comparable.

Appraisals people order here

Date-of-death and estate-trust appraisals are common for older hillside homes held for many years. Divorce appraisals come up for condos and townhomes. Pre-listing appraisals help sellers who face limited recent sales nearby, and pre-purchase appraisals help buyers check a price when there is little to compare. Because there are so few sales, a Standard report with an inspection is often the right choice, giving room to explain adjustments and comparable selection in detail.

Asked about Old Town

There are almost no sales near my Old Town home. How will you value it?

We look outward to Mission Hills, Bay Park and Point Loma for homes of similar age, size and lot type, and adjust for location. The report spells out why each sale was used and how it was adjusted.

Does the historic district affect my home's value?

The planned district limits what you can build or change. That can reduce redevelopment potential but also protects the setting. We compare to homes with similar restrictions to see how buyers treat them.

Is freeway noise a factor?

For homes near Interstates 5 or 8, yes. We compare against sales with similar exposure and note the distance and direction to the freeway.
