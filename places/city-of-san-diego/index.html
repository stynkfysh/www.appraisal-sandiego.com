City of San Diego

One city, more than 50 planning areas, and very different values block to block

The City of San Diego covers roughly 370 square miles, running from San Ysidro at the Mexican border up to the edge of Rancho Bernardo and from the Point Loma peninsula east to the hills above Tierrasanta and San Carlos. It is the second-largest city in California by population. For an appraiser, that size matters because a single city label tells you almost nothing about a house. A 1912 bungalow in North Park and a 2018 tract home in Del Sur share a city name, a tax rate base and little else.

That is why a useful appraisal here starts with the right comparison area, not the city as a whole. The city is divided into more than 50 community planning areas, each with its own plan, zoning overlays and housing history. Good comps usually come from the same planning area and the same era of construction. You get a value that holds up when an attorney, a CPA, the IRS or a judge looks at it, because the sales behind it are the ones a buyer in that pocket would actually weigh.

The housing stock

Housing in the city falls into rough bands by age. The urban core and the older streetcar suburbs (Golden Hill, North Park, University Heights, Mission Hills, Kensington) hold Victorians, Craftsman bungalows and 1920s Spanish Revival homes, many on narrow lots with canyon edges. The mid-century ring (Clairemont, Allied Gardens, Del Cerro, Bay Park, Serra Mesa, Paradise Hills) is mostly single-story ranch tracts from the 1950s and 1960s. Mira Mesa, Rancho Penasquitos, Scripps Ranch and Tierrasanta filled in during the 1970s and 1980s, often with HOAs. The newest planned areas, like Carmel Valley, Pacific Highlands Ranch, Black Mountain Ranch and Del Sur, carry master HOAs and frequently Mello-Roos. High-rise condos cluster downtown, in Little Italy and in Mission Valley.

Value drivers to watch around City of San Diego

01

The coastal height limit and coastal zone

Proposition D, passed by voters in 1972, caps most new buildings west of Interstate 5 at 30 feet. Add the California Coastal Zone permit rules, and you get limits on how much you can add to a beach-area house. When we value a coastal property, the realistic upside of remodeling or rebuilding is part of the picture, and it is not the same as the upside inland.

02

Canyons and fire hazard maps

Open-space canyons wind through almost every older part of the city. A lot on a canyon rim may give you privacy and a view, but it may also be mapped in a fire hazard zone rated Very High, and that changes what insurance costs and whether you can get it. We look at the slope, the usable yard, defensible space and how nearby rim sales were treated by buyers before giving that view a dollar figure.

03

Mello-Roos in the newer north

Many of the master-planned areas north of State Route 56 and around Black Mountain Ranch were financed with Community Facilities District bonds. Those special taxes show up on the property tax bill and can run several thousand dollars a year. Two similar houses a mile apart can trade differently if one carries a CFD and the other does not, so we check the bill on every comparable we use.

04

ADUs and lot-level zoning

San Diego has some of the most permissive accessory dwelling unit rules in the state, including its own ADU bonus program. A lot that can hold a second or third unit has a different buyer pool than one that cannot. We separate permitted, finished units from potential ones, because an appraisal has to value what is there and legal, not what might be built later.

Appraisals people order here

Across the city, the most frequent private assignments are date-of-death and estate-trust appraisals, since so many long-held homes in the older neighborhoods pass through trusts. Divorce appraisals are common for both condos and single-family houses, and Proposition 19 has pushed more families to get family-transfer values before deeding a house to a child. Tax appeal requests rise when assessed values outrun the market. When a house is being divided between parties or sold, people usually choose a Standard report that includes an interior inspection. A Desktop or Basic Desktop report often works for planning or for estates where the house was recently photographed and its condition is known.

Asked about City of San Diego

Do you appraise every neighborhood in the City of San Diego?

Yes. We cover the whole city, from San Ysidro and Otay Mesa to Rancho Bernardo, plus the coast and the eastern neighborhoods like San Carlos and Tierrasanta. The fee starts at the same published rate citywide. Larger lots, luxury homes, rush deadlines and court testimony can raise it, and you get a fixed quote before anything is scheduled.

How do you pick comparable sales in a city this large?

We start inside the same community planning area and the same construction era, then widen only when we have to. A comp across a freeway or in a newer tract is adjusted carefully or left out. The goal is to show the sales a typical buyer for your house would have looked at, and the report explains why each one was chosen.

Can you value a San Diego home for an earlier date?

Yes. Looking back in time is ordinary work, and these reports are routine for estates, divorces and tax matters. The comparables we choose sold near the effective date, and we rely on photos, listing histories and what the family tells us to judge how the home looked back then. Older effective dates can take more research, which the fixed quote reflects.
