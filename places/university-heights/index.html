University Heights

Mesa-top bungalows on the Mission Valley rim around Park Boulevard and Adams

The name comes from a plan that never happened. In the 1880s, developers set aside land here for a college that did not get built. In 1899, the State Normal School, the teachers college that later became San Diego State, opened on Park Boulevard. A cable car line ran to Mission Cliff Gardens at Park and Adams, which became a popular park.

Today, University Heights is a residential area of bungalows, small apartments and condos on the mesa above Mission Valley. Trolley Barn Park marks the old streetcar turnaround. For appraisal, location on the mesa matters. Homes on the northern rim can have valley views, while homes near Park Boulevard trade on walkability.

The housing stock

University Heights homes are mostly Craftsman and Spanish Revival bungalows from about 1905 to 1940, on modest lots. Some larger homes sit on the canyon rims. Postwar decades added apartment buildings and small multi-unit properties. There are condo conversions and newer condo projects along Park Boulevard and nearby streets. Many homes have detached garages, and ADUs are increasingly common. The area includes small historic districts and individually designated homes.

Value drivers to watch around University Heights

01

Mission Valley views

Homes on the northern rim of the mesa can look across Mission Valley toward the hills on the far side. A clear, wide view from the living areas adds value, and buyers pay attention to whether trees or newer buildings could block it. We compare to sales with similar views rather than to interior blocks.

02

Walkability

Homes within a few blocks of Park Boulevard and Adams Avenue have coffee shops, restaurants and Trolley Barn Park close by, and many buyers pay for that. Houses fronting the busier streets can face noise and parking pressure, which works the other way. We look at block-level location and compare to sales at similar distances.

03

Canyon slopes

Canyon lots on the valley rim offer privacy and views, but they can bring steep yards, retaining walls, drainage questions and placement in a fire hazard severity zone. Those affect insurance costs and the pool of buyers. We weigh the usable yard and the slope against nearby canyon-rim sales.

04

Small multi-unit properties

University Heights has a lot of duplexes, triplexes and fourplexes, many dating to the 1920s through the 1960s. Buyers for these look closely at rents and expenses as well as comparable sales. We use the 2-4 unit approach when it fits, so the report reflects how investors here actually price a building.

Appraisals people order here

Older homes owned for a long stretch often need an estate-trust or date-of-death appraisal. Divorce appraisals come up for houses and condos. Family-transfer appraisals help with Proposition 19. Owners of small multi-unit properties use 2-4 unit reports for buyouts and planning. Sellers of homes with views can use a pre-listing appraisal to set their price. For condos or standard bungalows with plenty of sales, a Desktop report can work. For view homes, canyon lots or multi-unit properties, a Standard report with an inspection is usually better.

Asked about University Heights

How do you value a view of Mission Valley?

We compare to sales with similar views and use the price difference as a guide. We note how wide the view is and whether trees or buildings block part of it.

Can you appraise a small apartment building here?

Yes. For two to four units, we offer Desktop and Standard 2-4 Unit reports. We look at sales and rents.

Does being near Park Boulevard affect value?

It can. Walkability is a plus for many buyers, but traffic and noise can be minuses. We compare to sales at similar distances from the main streets.
