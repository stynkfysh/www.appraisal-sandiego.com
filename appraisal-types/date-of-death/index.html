Date-of-Death Appraisal

Market value looking back to the date of death, documented so your CPA can use it.

When someone dies and leaves a home in San Diego County, the value on that date matters for years. The heirs use it as their starting cost basis, it is listed on the estate inventory, and your CPA will rely on that figure when the house finally sells. A date-of-death appraisal looks backward. Brian Ward looks at the home in its current state, then compares it with sales that closed close to when the owner died, so the value matches the market at that time rather than today's.

Who needs a date-of-death value

Executors, successor trustees and heirs are the usual clients, often at the request of a CPA or estate attorney. Sometimes the need is immediate because the family wants to list the house. Other times it surfaces years later, when a sibling sells and the tax preparer asks what the basis was. Either way the question is the same: what would this property have sold for on that specific day? A retrospective appraisal answers it with dated comparable sales, explained adjustments and a signed certification. That carries far more weight than an online estimate printed long after the fact.

Step-up in basis and why the date matters

Federal tax rules generally set the basis of inherited property at what it was worth on the open market when the owner died, which is commonly called a step-up. If your parents bought a Clairemont house in the 1970s for a small fraction of what it is worth now, the step-up can erase most of the taxable gain on a later sale. The catch is that someone has to document that value. Without a credible number, you may end up defending a guess if the IRS asks how the gain was calculated.

California adds a wrinkle. For community property held by a married couple, federal rules can allow both halves to step up when the first spouse dies, not only the decedent's half. An executor can also elect an alternate valuation date six months after death, but only when it lowers both the gross estate and the estate tax owed. These rules have conditions, so confirm how they apply to your family with your CPA or estate attorney before relying on them.

How Brian handles it in San Diego County

Brian has appraised homes across the county since 2004, from older bungalows in North Park to newer tract homes in Otay Ranch and rural parcels outside Ramona. For a date-of-death assignment he uses sales that closed before the effective date, so later price movement does not leak hindsight into the value. He asks the family what the house looked like then, what has been repaired, cleared out or replaced since, and adjusts the condition analysis to match. If the home has already been sold, he can work from the listing, photos and the closing record.

Property tax is a separate question

A death is generally a change in ownership for property tax purposes, so the San Diego County Assessor will reassess the home unless an exclusion applies, such as a transfer to a surviving spouse or a qualifying parent-child transfer under Proposition 19. The Assessor sets its own value for that purpose. Your appraisal is prepared for basis and estate reporting, but a credible value tied to the date of death is useful if the new assessment looks high and you decide to ask for a review.

Questions about this appraisal

How far back can the effective date go?

There is no fixed limit. Brian can value a home as of a date months or many years in the past, as long as reliable sales data exists for that period. The older the effective date, the more I depend on records and what the family remembers about condition, so those assignments may take a bit more time. Give him the date when you call and he will confirm the data supports it before you commit.

Do I need this if no estate tax is owed?

Usually, yes. Most estates owe no federal estate tax, but the basis question still matters to anyone who later sells the home. The step-up resets the cost basis, and the IRS can ask you to support it when a capital gain is reported. A report prepared close to the time of death is much easier to defend than a value reconstructed years later. Ask your CPA what your situation calls for.

The house already sold. Can you still appraise it?

Yes. Brian can complete a retrospective appraisal using the listing, the closing record, any photos you have and public data. A sale soon after death is useful evidence, but it is not automatically the date-of-death value, especially if the house was cleaned up, repaired or sold months later in a different market. The report explains how that sale was weighed alongside the other comparables.
