Divorce Appraisal

Neutral home values for dividing community property in a California divorce.

In most California divorces the house is the largest asset on the table, and the number assigned to it shapes every trade that follows. If one spouse keeps the home, the value decides how much equity the other is bought out for. If it is sold, the value helps set a realistic price and a timeline. Brian Ward prepares divorce appraisals for San Diego County homes as a neutral appraiser. He is not hired to push the number up or down, and the report says so.

Who orders a divorce appraisal

Family law attorneys order most of them, sometimes jointly for both parties and sometimes for one side. Mediators use them to give both spouses the same starting point. Spouses representing themselves order them when they want a number they can trust before signing a settlement. A joint engagement is often the cheapest path, since it can replace two competing reports. When the parties cannot agree on one appraiser, each side may hire its own, and Brian's report is written to hold up if the other side's appraiser reviews it.

Community property and the valuation date

California is a community property state. Property acquired during the marriage is generally community property, while property owned before marriage or received by gift or inheritance is generally separate. A house can be both: bought before the marriage but paid down with community earnings, for example. In those cases attorneys may apportion interests using formulas courts have developed, and they need values at specific dates to do the math. Brian supplies the values; your attorney applies the formulas.

The date of separation matters for many issues, but the home itself is usually valued as near the trial or settlement date as practical, unless the court orders another date for good cause. Some cases also need a value as of the purchase date, the date of marriage or the date of a refinance. Brian can provide a current value and one or more retrospective values in the same assignment. Your attorney decides which dates apply, so ask before you order.

How Brian approaches it in San Diego County

Brian treats both spouses the same. He schedules the inspection with whoever has access, notes any repairs or improvements either party points out, and lets the comparable sales decide the value. In parts of the county with a lot of remodeled flips, like Clairemont, Bay Park or Encanto, the gap between an original-condition home and a remodeled one can be large, so he documents condition carefully and shows the comparables used for each adjustment. If a pool, ADU or garage conversion lacks permits, he notes it and explains how it was handled.

Improvements made after separation can raise questions about who paid for what. Brian does not decide those reimbursement issues, but he can describe each improvement, when it appears to have been made, and how the market treats it. That gives the attorneys the facts they need to argue credits and charges, while the appraisal itself stays neutral on who should receive them. If a spouse claims an improvement added value, the report can show whether the market agrees.

Choosing the right report

A Standard appraisal with a full interior inspection is the usual choice for divorce, because a settlement or a judge will rely on it and condition differences can swing the value. When one spouse will not allow access, a Drive-By appraisal of the exterior can fill the gap. For an early planning number, before either side commits to a buyout, a Desktop or Basic Desktop report gives a credible range at a lower cost.

Questions about this appraisal

Can one appraiser work for both of us?

Yes, and it often saves money and time. With a joint engagement, both spouses or both attorneys are named as intended users, both can provide information, and both receive the same report. Brian does not take instructions from one side about the result. If you later want a second opinion, the other party is free to hire another appraiser or ask for a review.

My spouse will not let the appraiser inside. What happens?

Brian can complete a Drive-By appraisal based on the exterior, public records, prior listing photos and permit history. The report will state that the interior was not inspected and that condition was assumed. If access becomes available later, he can update the report. Your attorney may also be able to seek a court order allowing an inspection.

Can the value be set as of the date we separated?

Yes. Brian prepares retrospective appraisals using sales that closed around the date your attorney specifies. Whether the separation date is the right valuation date depends on the issue being decided, and California courts usually value the home closer to trial unless there is good cause otherwise. Confirm the dates with your attorney before ordering.

If your case ends up in trial, will you appear as a witness?

Brian can provide deposition or trial testimony about his appraisal. Time spent preparing and giving testimony is charged apart from the appraisal fee, with terms settled in advance. Many divorces settle once both sides see a credible value, so testimony is not always needed. Let him know when you order if a trial date is already set.

Usually the right fit

The Standard appraisal ($699 single-family, $799 for 2-4 units) fits most divorce cases, since condition differences can swing value and the report may be scrutinized. A Drive-By ($649) works when interior access is refused. A Desktop ($449) or Basic Desktop ($299) can serve as an early planning figure before settlement talks.
