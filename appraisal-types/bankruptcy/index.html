Bankruptcy Appraisal

A documented home value for your bankruptcy schedules, exemptions and plan.

In a bankruptcy, the value of your home is not just a line on a form. It decides how much equity you have, whether that equity is protected by an exemption, and in Chapter 13 how much your plan may have to pay unsecured creditors. Trustees look closely at that number. Brian Ward prepares bankruptcy appraisals for San Diego County homes, usually as of the petition filing date, with the supporting sales laid out so your attorney can explain the value to the trustee.

Who needs a bankruptcy appraisal

Debtors' attorneys order most bankruptcy appraisals, either before filing to plan the case or after a trustee questions the scheduled value. Chapter 7 trustees sometimes order their own to test whether there is equity to sell. Creditors with a lien may want one in a valuation dispute. If you are considering bankruptcy and own a home, the appraisal is one of the first things your attorney will want, because the equity calculation shapes which chapter makes sense and what you can expect to keep.

Timing matters too. Chapter 13 plans run three to five years, and value can come up again if you later sell, refinance or modify the plan. A well-documented appraisal from the start of the case makes those later conversations easier, because everyone can see what the home was like and what the market showed on the petition date. It also shows the trustee that the value was taken seriously from day one.

Chapter 7, Chapter 13 and the homestead exemption

In Chapter 7, a trustee can sell property to pay creditors if there is equity above the liens, sale costs and your exemption. In Chapter 13 you generally keep the home, but the plan usually has to pay unsecured creditors at least what they would have received in a Chapter 7 liquidation. Either way, a higher value means more nonexempt equity, which is why the number matters. Even a modest change in value can shift that result.

California makes debtors choose between two exemption systems. The system in Code of Civil Procedure section 704 includes a homestead exemption that, since 2021, is tied to county median home prices within a floor and ceiling that are adjusted for inflation. The other system has a much smaller homestead amount but a broader wildcard. Which one fits depends on your whole financial picture, and only your bankruptcy attorney should make that call.

How Brian handles bankruptcy work in San Diego County

Brian values the home as of the date your attorney specifies, usually the petition date, and uses sales that support that date. He documents condition honestly, including deferred maintenance, roof or foundation issues and anything else a buyer would discount for, because a trustee's own broker may focus only on the best comparables. In neighborhoods like Santee, El Cajon or Oceanside, where nearby remodeled sales can look much higher than an untouched house, the condition analysis carries real weight. He does not shade values to fit an exemption; the report has to stand on the data.

Which report works best

A Standard appraisal with an interior inspection is usually the right tool when equity is close to the exemption line or the trustee has already raised questions, because it documents condition directly. When the numbers are clearly on one side, a Desktop report may be enough to support the schedules. Rental duplexes and fourplexes use the 2-4 Unit versions. If the petition date is in the past, the work is retrospective and the quote will reflect that.

Fees start at $449 for a Desktop and $699 for a Standard single-family appraisal. The quote is fixed before the inspection is scheduled, which helps your attorney plan the case budget and keeps the cost predictable during a stressful time. Retrospective petition dates, rush deadlines and multi-unit properties can raise the fee, and you will know that before you commit. Your attorney receives the report directly if you name them as an intended user.

Questions about this appraisal

Is it better to have the appraisal done before filing, or after?

Many attorneys like to have it done before filing, so the schedules show a value with support behind it and the case strategy rests on actual equity numbers. An appraisal can also be ordered afterward, as of the petition date, if a trustee challenges the scheduled value. Ask your bankruptcy attorney which timing fits your case. Brian can work either way.

Will the trustee accept your appraisal?

No appraiser can promise that. Trustees review reports and sometimes get their own broker opinions or appraisals. What a well-supported report does is give your attorney concrete evidence to discuss: the comparables, the adjustments and the condition photos. When two values differ, the one with clearer support usually shapes the conversation.

Can you lower the value so my equity is protected?

No. Brian is bound by appraisal standards to give an independent, unbiased opinion, and a slanted report would hurt your case once a trustee looked at it. What he can do is make sure real problems, such as an old roof, foundation cracks or an outdated interior, are documented and reflected in the value instead of being overlooked.

Do I need to attend the visit?

Brian only needs someone who can open the door, which usually takes under an hour for a typical house. Point out anything that needs repair; he will photograph it. If the home is tenant-occupied, arrange access with the tenant in advance. The inspection is focused on the property, not on your personal belongings or finances.

Usually the right fit

The Standard appraisal ($699, or $799 for 2-4 units) usually fits, because condition evidence is what separates a well-supported value from a trustee's broker opinion. A Desktop ($449) can support schedules when equity is clearly above or below the exemption. Retrospective petition dates may add to the fee.
